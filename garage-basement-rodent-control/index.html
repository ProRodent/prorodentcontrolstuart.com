Why the garage is where it starts.

Basements are rare in Stuart because of the high water table, so the garage does the work of a basement. It holds holiday boxes, spare refrigerators, pet food, paint, tools and the water heater. It also has the largest opening in the house, a door that is raised and lowered every day, and a bottom seal that wears with every cycle.

Rodents treat a garage like an annex. Warm, sheltered and full of cardboard, it offers nesting material and quiet. If the garage connects to the house through a door with a gap at the bottom, or through a shared wall with pipes and wiring, the animals do not stay in the garage for long.

What we check first.

We begin with the garage door itself. The bottom seal should meet the slab with no light visible underneath. The side tracks and the top corners are worth a look, since rodents squeeze through warped gaps at each end. We then check the shared wall with the house, the penetrations at the water heater and the gap around any garage-side utility entry.

  • Bottom seal and side seals on the garage door.
  • Gaps where the door frame meets the slab.
  • Shared wall penetrations and the interior door threshold.
  • Water heater closet, laundry hookups and utility panels.
  • Stored cardboard, fabric and pet food on the floor.
  • Overhead storage racks and ceiling gaps.

Droppings along the base of shelves, shredded boxes, chewed pet food bags and greasy marks near the door corners tell us the route. A rat may nest in stored cloth, while mice tuck into the folds of boxes.

Removal and storage changes.

Traps go along the base of walls and shelving, behind the water heater and at the door corners. Because garages have pets and children coming through, we place them in out-of-reach positions or inside protective boxes, and we tell you where they are.

Storage changes make a visible difference. Move cardboard boxes into lidded plastic bins and lift them off the floor. Keep pet food and birdseed in sealed metal or hard plastic containers. Keep a clear stripe along the walls so that you can see signs early and so traps have room.

A clear garage wall is easier to inspect, trap and keep clean.

Sealing the garage envelope.

A new bottom seal or threshold is a quick and effective upgrade. We also seal gaps around pipes, conduit and line sets with mesh and sealant, and screen any vent openings. The shared wall with the house is the last line of defense, so gaps there get attention first. Our exclusion and sealing page explains the materials.

Hurricane preparation changes garages too. Shutters, tape and stored supplies bring boxes and bags into the space for weeks. After the storm, check for new openings, and clear out wet cardboard, which rodents readily use for nests.

Storage, appliances and wiring.

A second refrigerator or chest freezer in the garage can run warm behind and attract mice to the heat. Wiring in the garage ceiling and behind appliances is also a target. Chewed insulation on cables causes shorts, tripped breakers and, in the worst case, fires. If you find chewed wiring, leave it alone and mention it on the call.

Pool equipment sometimes sits in or beside the garage. Pump motors and timers invite nesting, and chewed lines can cause costly repairs. We include a look at that equipment in the visit.

A monthly garage routine.

  • Look along the base of each wall for fresh droppings.
  • Check the door seal for light, tears and gaps at the corners.
  • Lift a few stored boxes and look underneath.
  • Confirm that pet food and seed are in sealed containers.
  • Glance behind the water heater, the fridge and the freezer.

Five minutes a month is enough to catch most problems early. Write the date on a sticky note by the door, so the habit survives busy months.

Garages converted to living space.

Many Stuart garages have been partly converted into workshops, studios or extra rooms. Those spaces mix human use with the gaps of a garage, and they are popular with mice because of the warmth and the stored materials.

If part of your garage has been finished, tell us. The finished walls hide voids that need a different approach than open framing, and we plan the inspection to reach them.

Older garages and detached structures.

Detached garages, workshops and sheds behind older Stuart homes are often wood frame on a slab or piers, with gaps at the sill plate and under the door. We check them as part of the visit and note any that need more than a seal. Backyard and outdoor rodent control covers the yard side of the same problem.

Call with the street address and a short description of what is stored in the garage. We bring the right materials for the job and plan the visit around your schedule.

Before we arrive

  • Clear a path to the attic hatch, garage wall and the area under the kitchen sink.
  • Leave droppings undisturbed; protective cleanup is part of the visit.
  • Avoid setting your own snap traps in a spot children can reach.
  • Note any smell, since odor can point to where an animal has died.

Questions about garage and basement rodent control

Do you work on basements too?

True basements are uncommon in Stuart. If your property has one, the same inspection and sealing approach applies to it.

Will a new door seal solve it?

It closes a common entry, but not necessarily all of them. We inspect the whole garage envelope, then decide.

Can you handle detached garages and sheds?

Yes. We check them during the same visit and include them in the plan.
