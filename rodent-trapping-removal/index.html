Why placement beats quantity.

People who try trapping on their own often set a dozen traps in a dozen random spots and catch nothing. Rodents are creatures of habit. They follow edges, return to the same routes each night, and avoid open floor. A single well-placed trap on the right run does more than ten placed by guess.

A technician finds those runs first. Greasy rub marks on walls and rafters, droppings in lines, and polished paths in dust show where the animals move. Traps go at those points, which is why trapping follows an inspection and not the other way around.

The trap types we use.

Different animals and spaces call for different tools. In attics, snap traps and enclosed multi-catch devices work along rafters. In kitchens and cabinets, small snap traps tucked behind plumbing do the job. Outdoors, covered stations protect traps from weather and keep non-target animals out.

Situation.Typical approach.
Mice in a cabinet or wall.Small snap traps against the wall, trigger end to the edge.
Roof rats in an attic.Snap traps on rafters and along runs, set after an acclimation period.
Norway rats at a burrow.Covered stations beside the burrow and along fence lines.
Garage with stored items.Traps along the base of shelving and at the door corners.

We do not leave loose poison where children, pets or wildlife can reach it. If bait stations are part of the plan, they are locked, tamper-resistant and placed deliberately. Federal rules keep the strongest products for professional use for good reason.

Checks and the schedule.

A trap that is not checked is a trap that fails. After setting, we return on a schedule that fits the situation, usually within a few days, to clear catches, reset traps and note which locations were active. That record guides where to focus sealing.

A drop in catches over two or three visits means the population is falling. If catches stay steady, there is still a way in, and we widen the inspection. This is why trapping and exclusion and sealing go together.

Catches that stop are good news. Catches that continue mean a gap is still open.

Where Stuart conditions matter.

Roof rats are wary and need time. They may ignore a new trap for days, so we sometimes place traps unset first. Humidity and heat also affect bait, so fresh bait is rotated often. In the rainy months, outdoor stations need to be sheltered and checked after storms.

Stuart's waterfront lots, fruit trees and palm cover bring steady rat pressure, while downtown storefronts and older cottages bring mouse and rat calls tied to food waste and shared walls. We adjust the plan to the property rather than applying one template.

Disposal, safety and what you handle.

We remove and dispose of catches, so you do not need to touch them. Wear gloves if you ever must handle a trap, avoid bare hands and keep traps away from children and pets. For cleanup after removal see attic and crawl space cleanup or dead rodent removal and odor control.

Public health agencies recommend sealing entry points, trapping, and cleaning up, in that overall approach. We follow it because it works. Trapping clears the animals inside, and sealing keeps new ones from arriving.

Common trapping mistakes at home.

  • Setting traps in open floor space instead of against walls.
  • Using too little bait or bait that has dried out in the heat.
  • Handling traps with bare hands, which leaves human scent.
  • Checking too rarely, so a catch sits for days.
  • Trapping without sealing, which lets new animals replace the old ones.

Each of these is easy to fix once you know it. The last one is the most common, and it explains why a home can seem to have an endless supply of mice or rats. Nothing is wrong with the traps. The door is simply still open.

Trapping in occupied homes with pets and children.

Households with pets and children need extra planning. Traps go in locations that small hands and paws cannot reach, such as inside covered stations, high on rafters or behind appliances. We tell you where each one is, and we count them in and out on each visit.

If you have a curious dog or cat, mention it. We can use enclosed designs and place them in areas the pet cannot enter, so the trapping works without added risk.

Before we arrive

  • Keep everyone away from visible nesting material and do not shake out boxes.
  • Skip bleach or sprays on dry droppings until the plan is set.
  • Do not seal a hole yourself before the visit; closing it with an animal inside causes new problems.
  • Have a rough idea of how long the activity has been going on.

Questions about rodent trapping and removal

How many visits does trapping take?

It depends on the infestation. Many jobs take two to four visits across a couple of weeks, with the last visit confirming that catches have stopped.

Is it safe with pets in the house?

Traps and stations are placed out of reach of pets and children, and we explain where they are. Tell us about any animals so we can plan placement.

Why not just use glue boards?

Glue boards are inhumane and unreliable on rats, and they can trap non-target animals. We use snap traps and stations designed for the species.
