Why the inspection comes first.

Every good rodent job starts the same way, by finding out what is actually going on. A technician who sets traps or quotes a price before looking is guessing. An inspection replaces the guess with a record of where the animals travel, where they enter and how long the problem has probably been there.

In Stuart the answer varies widely from one property to the next. A tile-roofed house in the western neighborhoods has different gaps than a wood-frame cottage near downtown. A lot with a hedge against the wall and a palm over the roof has different pressure than a clear lot with a concrete apron.

What we walk through.

We start outside, because that is where the way in usually is. The walk follows the roofline, soffits, vents, tile edges and wall penetrations, then drops to the foundation line, garage door, utility entries and the yard. Inside, we check the attic, garage, kitchen and laundry, and any closet or cabinet with plumbing through the wall.

  • Roof edges, tile ends, ridge vents and gable screens.
  • Exterior wall penetrations for pipes, cable and line sets.
  • Garage door seals and slab edges.
  • Attic insulation, rafters and duct runs.
  • Kitchen and laundry cabinets with plumbing.
  • Yard hedges, palms, fruit trees, sheds and woodpiles.

We look for droppings, gnaw marks, rub marks, urine stains, nesting material and tracks in dust. Each type of sign tells a different story. Fresh droppings are dark and soft, while old ones are dry and pale, which gives a rough idea of how long activity has been going on.

What you get at the end.

You get a plain description of what was found, which species is likely responsible, and where they enter. Then we give a plan, usually trapping, sealing and cleanup, and a price for each part. You decide what to do. The estimate is not a sales script, and it is given before any work starts.

What the estimate covers.

  • Which animals are present and how far activity extends.
  • A list of entry points and how each would be sealed.
  • Trapping steps and the number of visits expected.
  • Cleanup or repair items, priced separately.
  • What the follow-up visit will check.
The price you hear matches what the inspection found.

Older and newer buildings.

The Downtown Stuart Historic District has structures that date to the early twentieth century, such as the Lyric Theatre from 1926. Old wood and masonry shift over decades, so gaps form at foundation lines, around pipes and where additions meet the original walls. Newer construction has its own habits, like unsealed line set penetrations and soffit gaps that appear when trades finish without a final seal.

We tell you which category your building fits and what that means for the plan. Sometimes the answer is that the house is tight and the activity is minor. In that case we say so, and you can avoid paying for work you do not need.

What an honest estimate should include.

Whoever you hire, ask for the same things. The animal identified, the entry points listed, the number of visits expected, the materials to be used and a separate price for any cleanup or repair. If a quote arrives as a single number with no explanation, ask what is in it.

Be wary of any provider who promises a result before looking, or who quotes a fixed price by phone for a problem that has not been seen. Rodent jobs vary too much for that to be honest. We say what we know, what we do not know yet and what the inspection will settle.

Inspections for sellers, buyers and managers.

Not every inspection follows a complaint. Sellers sometimes want to know the condition of a house before listing it, buyers want a second opinion on a flagged item, and property managers want a record for a building. We handle those the same way, with a walk, a written summary and a plan.

If you need a document for a transaction, say so on the call, and we will tell you what we can provide.

Before we arrive

  • Keep everyone away from visible nesting material and do not shake out boxes.
  • Skip bleach or sprays on dry droppings until the plan is set.
  • Do not seal a hole yourself before the visit; closing it with an animal inside causes new problems.
  • Have a rough idea of how long the activity has been going on.

Questions about rodent inspection and estimates

How long does an inspection take?

Most inspections take under an hour for a typical house, though larger lots or complex attics take longer. We give you a plain summary before leaving.

Do I get the price before work starts?

Yes. The plan and the price are explained after the inspection and before anything begins.

What if you find nothing?
