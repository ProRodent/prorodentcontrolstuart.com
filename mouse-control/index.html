What a mouse call looks like in Stuart.

A mouse call usually starts with a sound or a small mess. Light scratching behind a cabinet, a rustle in a closet after midnight, or a scatter of dark pellets in a drawer. In Stuart the picture has one extra feature that colder states do not share, which is that mice never get a season off. Warm weather keeps them breeding in every month, so a problem that starts in October can look like a colony by spring.

The first thing a technician does is confirm the animal. A young roof rat or Norway rat can leave similar droppings, and the plan differs. House mouse droppings are roughly the size of a grain of rice. If the droppings are larger or spindle shaped, we widen the inspection to include rats, and you will find that covered on our rat control page.

House mice fit through a gap about a quarter inch wide, roughly the width of a pencil. That is a smaller opening than most people expect. A garage door seal that has worn flat, a gap where a refrigerant line enters the wall, or a dryer vent without a working flap are all large enough.

Where mice get in around Stuart homes.

Most Stuart homes are concrete block with stucco, set on a slab. That construction has few cracks where mice can squeeze through the main wall, but it has plenty of penetrations. Air conditioner line sets, plumbing stub-outs under sinks, electrical conduit, and the gap between the garage door and the slab are the usual routes.

Older wood-frame cottages, the kind found in and around downtown, offer a different set of openings. Wood siding meets the foundation in uneven lines, crawl space vents lose their screens, and old pipe chases leave holes behind appliances. We look at the exterior first, then work inward.

  • Garage door bottom seals and the corners where the door meets the slab.
  • Line set and plumbing penetrations in exterior walls.
  • Weep holes at the base of brick or stucco veneer.
  • Dryer, bath fan and range hood vents with missing or bent flaps.
  • Soffit returns and gaps beside roof tiles.
A mouse needs a gap the width of a pencil, and every home has a few.

How we remove mice.

Snap traps and tamper-resistant bait stations go on the routes we actually find, not scattered at random. Mice hug walls and follow the same path nightly, so a trap placed a foot off that path catches little. We position traps with the trigger end against the wall, in attics along rafters, in cabinets behind plumbing, and in garages along the base of shelving.

We do not rely on loose poison inside the living space. A mouse that eats bait can die inside a wall, where it causes odor and flies for weeks. If bait is appropriate, it goes in a locked station out of reach of children and pets, and the plan says why. For removal in a specific room, see rodent trapping and removal.

Keeping them out for good.

Removing the mice already inside only resets the clock if the entry points stay open. After the traps do their work, we close the gaps with materials mice cannot chew. Steel wool alone rots in humid air, so we use stainless mesh, sheet metal, copper mesh and a masonry-grade sealant matched to each opening. Our exclusion and entry point sealing page lists the materials by location.

A new door sweep is often the single cheapest fix on the whole job. Garage door seals warp in Stuart's heat, and a replacement seal that meets the slab flush takes minutes to install and closes one of the top entry points.

After the visit.

What to watch for after treatment.

  • No fresh droppings in the same spot for at least a week.
  • No scratching at night in the same wall or ceiling area.
  • Traps staying empty on the follow-up visit.
  • No new gnaw marks on boxes or packaging.

If activity continues, call. We would rather return and recheck than leave a half-finished fix. Keep pantry food in sealed containers, store pet food overnight in a lidded bin, and trim shrubs back from the wall so there is no covered path to the house.

Mouse problems that look like something else.

Not every noise or smell is a mouse. A clicking or ticking in the ceiling on hot afternoons is often framing and ductwork expanding in the sun. A faint scratch in a wall can be a loose pipe strap. Mice also get blamed for damage that belongs to roof rats, which are heavier and leave larger gnaw marks.

The reverse happens too. A few droppings in a drawer can look like a trivial leftover, when they are really the leading edge of an established nest in the wall behind it. We would rather spend ten minutes confirming the animal than set traps for the wrong one.

If you are unsure, take a photo of the droppings next to a coin, note when the sound starts and stops, and mention both on the call. Those two details settle most questions before we arrive.

Mice in rentals, condos and townhomes.

Shared buildings add a wrinkle. Mice move along shared walls and utility chases, so treating one unit can push activity into the next. If you rent or live in a condo or townhome, tell us who manages the building and whether neighbors have noticed anything.

Exterior sealing in a shared building usually needs the owner or association to approve it. We explain what can be done from inside the unit and what needs the building manager, so the conversation with them is specific.

Questions about mouse control

How quickly can mice multiply?

A female house mouse can have a litter roughly every three weeks under good conditions, which is why a small problem can grow quickly. Early action matters more than waiting to see if it passes.

Are mouse droppings dangerous to touch?

They can carry bacteria, so do not sweep or vacuum them dry. Wet them with a disinfectant and wipe them up with gloves, following CDC cleanup steps, or wait for the visit.

Do you treat mice and rats on the same visit?

Yes. If the inspection shows both, the plan covers both, and you hear the scope and price before work starts.
