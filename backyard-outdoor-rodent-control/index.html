The yard is where it starts.

Most homes get rodents from outside. The animals live in burrows, hedges, palms, woodpiles and sheds, then move indoors when the weather, food or pressure pushes them. If you only treat the house, the yard keeps supplying new arrivals.

Stuart yards give them plenty to work with. Mild weather means plants fruit and seed year-round. Landscaping stays dense, with palms, hibiscus, thick groundcover and hedges that touch walls. Waterfront and canal lots add more cover along the water edge.

Signs outdoors.

Burrows are the clearest sign of Norway rats. Look for openings about two to three inches across beside slabs, decks, sheds and under thick groundcover, often with smooth, packed soil at the edge. Roof rats are harder to spot on the ground. Look for gnawed fruit, with the skin chewed and the pulp hollowed, droppings under trees, and greasy rub marks on fence tops and pipes.

  • Burrow openings beside slabs, walls, sheds and decks.
  • Gnawed citrus, mango, avocado or palm seed.
  • Droppings under trees, on fence tops or in planters.
  • Rub marks along fences, pipes and rafters.
  • Chewed pet food bags and bird feeders.
  • Nests in stacked boxes, tarps or palm fronds.

Food, water and shelter.

Rodents need three things, and the yard gives all three. Food comes from fallen fruit, pet bowls, bird feeders and trash. Water comes from irrigation, dripping spigots, pet water bowls and standing water in planters. Shelter comes from dense vegetation, stacked material and structures that touch the ground.

Reducing any of these helps. Harvest or pick up fruit weekly. Bring pet food in at night. Use trash cans with tight lids, and keep them away from the house wall. Trim ground-level vegetation so that sunlight reaches the base of walls and fence lines, and rats lose cover.

Trim, tidy, then trap. In that order the yard stops feeding the house.

The line between yard and house.

Outdoor work only goes so far if the building is open. We check the roofline where branches touch, the foundation edges and any pipe or cable that enters the wall, then close those openings through exclusion and sealing. A hedge that touches the wall is a highway, and cutting it back by a foot or two breaks it.

Palms need special care. Old fronds that hang on the trunk form a sheltered nest that roof rats use. Having palms trimmed by a qualified tree service removes the nest and lets you see the trunk. Avoid doing the work yourself if the tree is tall.

What not to do in the yard.

Do not scatter loose bait around the yard. Pets, wildlife and children can reach it, and poisoned rats die in places you cannot see. Do not fill a burrow before you know it is empty, since animals dig out or find a new route toward the house.

Avoid heavy mulch against the foundation, and do not stack firewood against a wall. Both give rats cover close to the building. Move them a foot or two out and off the ground, and the pressure on the house drops.

Pools, lanais and outdoor kitchens.

Outdoor living areas are a big part of Florida life, and rodents take advantage. A pool pump enclosure, an outdoor kitchen with a cabinet below and a screened lanai with a gap at the bottom all offer shelter and sometimes food.

We check those spaces on a yard visit. Closing a cabinet gap or adding a screen to a vent takes minutes and removes a place to nest. Keep food scraps and drinks cleaned up after outdoor meals.

Seasons, storms and pools.

Heavy rain in the wet season can flood burrows and push animals toward buildings. After a big storm, check the foundation line and the garage for new activity. Pool equipment and screened lanais also see visitors, especially if food or drink is left out in the evening.

Natural habitat, such as the coastal cover near the Florida Oceanographic Coastal Center, comes close to many Stuart homes. Properties that back onto wooded or mangrove edges see more outdoor pressure, so we note what the lot borders and plan the burrow, shed and hedge checks around it.

Describe your yard when you call, including any fruit trees, palms, sheds, a pool or a shared fence. That lets us plan the visit before we arrive.

Questions about backyard and outdoor rodent control

Will cutting my hedges help?

Yes. Pulling vegetation back from walls and the roof removes cover and bridges, which makes the building a less attractive target.

Do you fill burrows?

Yes, once we confirm they are empty and the animals have been treated, so we do not seal anything inside.

Do bird feeders attract rats?

Spilled seed does. Use a catch tray, pick up what falls, and consider bringing feeders in at night.
