A home can look perfect and still have rodents. A short checklist helps you spot problems before you sign.

Buying or renting a home is stressful, and a pest problem is one more surprise nobody wants. Rodents hide, and a house that has been staged or cleaned for showings can conceal evidence. The good news is that most signs can be found with a careful walk and a few honest questions.

This guide gives you a checklist to use during showings, inspections and the first days after you move in. It is written for Stuart conditions, where year-round warmth, palms, tile roofs and block construction shape the usual weak points. It is not a substitute for a professional inspection, but it helps you know what to ask.

Questions to ask first.

Ask the owner or agent directly whether the home has had rodents, and whether any pest control has been done. A seller may have a disclosure obligation, so ask for any records. If a prior treatment occurred, ask what was done and when. A recent job with sealing is a good sign, while a history of repeated treatments with no sealing is a warning.

Ask about recent roof work, storm repairs and tree trimming. Those events open gaps and move animals. Ask whether any palms or fruit trees on the lot have been cleaned regularly. A neglected palm next to the roof is a risk.

If you are renting, ask how pest issues are handled and who pays. Get the answer in writing if possible, since a vague answer is hard to enforce later.

The garage and the exterior.

Start outside. Walk around the house and look at the roofline, the vents and the base of the walls. Look for gaps at the ends of tile, gable vents without mesh, soffit panels that are loose or stained and holes around pipes and the air conditioner line set. Rub marks, droppings or burrows at the base of walls are warning signs.

Inspect the garage door. If you can see light under it when closed, mice can enter. Check the corners and the seal. Look at the walls inside the garage for droppings, gnawed boxes and shredded material. A garage full of stored items from a previous owner is a place to look carefully.

  • Tile ends, vents and soffits at the roofline.
  • Gaps around pipes, line sets and the dryer vent.
  • Garage door seal and corners.
  • Droppings along walls, shelves and the water heater closet.
  • Burrows, gnawed fruit and rub marks in the yard.
  • Palms, branches or vines touching the roof.

The attic and the ceilings.

If you can, look in the attic from the hatch. Droppings on the insulation, flattened trails, shredded material and a sharp smell are clear signs. Notice any daylight that should not be there. Look at ductwork, since torn wrap can mean gnawing.

Inside the house, look at ceilings for stains and at vents for dark smears. Listen during a showing. If the house is quiet, a scratch may stand out. If you view in the evening, you have a better chance of hearing something.

A home inspection should include the attic. Ask the inspector to note any signs of rodents, and to look at the roofline and the crawl space or foundation. If anything is flagged, get a pest company to look before closing.

Kitchen, pantry and cabinets.

Open the cabinets under the sink and look at the back wall. Gaps around pipes are common entry points. Check for droppings, gnaw marks and greasy smears. Pull out the stove and the fridge if the owner allows, or at least look at the edges.

Check the pantry shelves and the corners. Droppings are easiest to see against a light surface. A sharp, musty smell in a closed cabinet can mean urine. A fresh coat of paint or a new plug-in air freshener in an odd place may be a cover.

Look at the laundry area as well. The gap behind the washer and the dryer vent are common routes. A mouse can enter through a vent flap that does not close.

Red flags and what they mean.

One dropping does not mean an infestation. A pattern does. Multiple droppings, fresh gnaw marks, a strong smell, noises or visible damage suggest a current problem. Tell the seller or landlord and ask for it to be dealt with before you move in.

A smell that is covered by fragrance is a particular warning. So is a house that has been recently painted in one room only. Ask why. A fresh repair near a vent, a pipe or a ceiling may hide an old problem.

Red flags worth a professional look.

  • Several fresh droppings in one area.
  • Gnawing on wood, wiring or stored boxes.
  • Greasy rub marks along walls, pipes or rafters.
  • A strong urine or rotting smell.
  • Evidence of nesting material in the attic, garage or cabinets.

If you move in and find signs.

Do not panic. Photograph what you see and note the date. If you rent, tell the landlord in writing. If you bought, check your inspection report and any seller disclosures. Then call a professional for an inspection.

Moving also stirs animals up. Boxes in the garage, stacked cardboard and empty rooms give them shelter. Break down boxes quickly and keep food stored in sealed containers from day one.

A first-week routine after moving in.

  • Walk the garage and note anything unusual on day one.
  • Look under each sink and behind the washer.
  • Listen for movement in the ceilings in the evening.
  • Store food in sealed containers from the start.
  • Check the yard for burrows, fruit and rub marks.

These checks take minutes and give you a baseline. If something changes later, you will know it is new.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Soak the droppings and the surface in disinfectant first. Wiping comes only after the soak time.
  • Double check that waste is bagged and sealed, and never pick up a dead rodent with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Sources and standards

Public guidance behind the advice on this page. It describes general practice and rules, not a claim about any one provider.

  • CDC: How to Clean Up After Rodents: The public-health steps for airing out a space, wetting droppings with disinfectant instead of sweeping, and bagging waste.
  • University of Florida IFAS EDIS: Rat and Mouse Control (ENY-224): UF/IFAS Extension publication on identifying and controlling rats and mice in Florida homes (1997 revision; the biology and exclusion principles still apply).

Common questions

Should I add a pest inspection to a home purchase?

It is a reasonable step, especially for older homes. Ask your inspector to note rodent signs, and call a pest company if anything is flagged.

Who pays for rodent control in a rental?

It depends on the lease and local rules. Ask before you sign and get the answer in writing.

Can a fresh coat of paint hide a rodent problem?

It can hide stains and smells for a time. If a single room was painted recently, ask why and look closely.
