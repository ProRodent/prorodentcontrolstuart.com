Downtown Stuart has the kind of streetscape that people come to see. The Lyric Theatre has stood there since 1926, and the historic district around it holds early to mid twentieth century storefronts, offices and homes. Those buildings have character, and they also have age. Age leaves openings.

This guide looks at where rodents get into older buildings, why the pattern differs from newer construction and how owners can close gaps without damaging what makes the buildings worth keeping. It applies to downtown storefronts and also to older wood-frame homes elsewhere in Stuart.

How old buildings develop gaps.

Buildings move. Foundations settle, wood shrinks and swells with humidity, and masonry cracks. Over decades, small movements open joints and cracks that were once tight. Repairs and additions add new seams where old and new walls meet, and those seams are rarely perfect.

Utilities add holes. A building that has been wired, plumbed and re-wired over many decades has penetrations for old services that are no longer used. Pipes that once carried gas or water, cables for old telephone and television lines and abandoned conduit may all leave openings behind.

Maintenance matters too. A vent screen that rusted out ten years ago, a door sweep worn flat and a hole behind a refrigerator that no one remembers all form part of the picture. Each is small, and together they give rodents plenty to work with.

Shared walls and joints between buildings.

A row of storefronts often shares walls, with small gaps where one building meets the next. Rodents use the gaps, the shared ceiling voids and the space above a dropped ceiling to move from one unit to another. A problem in a restaurant may show up in a shop next door.

The roof is another connector. Parapets, flashing and the junctions between roofs create routes. Roof rats run along them, using cables, awnings and any vegetation that reaches the roofline.

Because the buildings are connected, the fix works best when neighbors cooperate. A single seal in one unit does not stop animals that enter through another. We explain this on every commercial and downtown visit, and we recommend sharing information with adjacent owners.

Common entry points in older masonry and wood.

  • Foundation vents with missing or rusted screens.
  • Gaps where pipes, conduit and old cables pass through walls.
  • Cracked mortar and open joints at the base of masonry.
  • Gaps beneath rear doors and at the edges of old door frames.
  • Openings at roof edges, parapet flashing and chimney bases.
  • Spaces around old coal chutes, dumbwaiters and abandoned vents.

Old wood buildings have their own list. Siding that meets the foundation in uneven lines, porch additions that never joined tightly and crawl space access panels with gaps all invite animals. The weep holes in later brick veneers are another point, and they should be screened, not sealed.

Sealing without damaging the building.

A historic building deserves a gentle approach. We do not drill, strip or hide original features without discussing it. Where gaps need closing, we choose materials that are compatible, such as metal mesh behind trim, cement patches matched to the masonry and replacement vent screens that fit the opening.

Heavy foam can do harm. It traps moisture, discolors masonry and is hard to remove. It is also soft enough for rodents to chew. We use it sparingly and only where it will be covered by something durable.

Owners of listed properties should check local requirements before significant changes. A pest visit is not a restoration, and we flag openings so that the right specialist can decide how to repair them. Our exclusion and entry point sealing page lists the materials and methods.

Inside: chases, ceilings and storage.

Older buildings are full of hidden space. Old chases run between floors, dropped ceilings cover original ones and storage rooms sit in basements and back corners. Rodents use all of them. A scratching sound above a ceiling in a shop often means a ceiling void with an easy path to the roof.

Storage habits are decisive. Cardboard against walls, food stored on the floor and cluttered back rooms give animals shelter. Clearing a path along walls and lifting items off the floor makes inspection easier and nesting harder.

Upstairs apartments share risk with the business below. Kitchens above restaurants see more activity, and tenants can only fix so much. Owners and tenants need to coordinate, and we explain what each can do.

A yearly walk-around for owners.

  • Check vent screens and weep holes for tears and rust.
  • Look for cracked mortar and open joints near the base.
  • Test door sweeps and frames at the rear.
  • Inspect roof edges and parapets for gaps.
  • Photograph anything that changed since last year.

Doing this once a year, ideally before the wet season, catches small gaps before they become routes. Keep the photos with your maintenance records.

How an inspection works in an old building.

We start outside. The rear, the roof and the foundation are the first stops. We look for gaps, rub marks and burrows at the base of the walls. Inside, we check storage rooms, kitchens, utility spaces and ceiling voids, and we follow pipes and wires to see where they pass through walls.

After the walk, we give a plan. It usually combines rodent trapping and removal with sealing, and for businesses it may include monitoring, as described on our commercial rodent control page. Residential owners of older homes may need rodent damage repair too.

Safe handling checklist for droppings and nests

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Never dry sweep or vacuum droppings, urine stains or nesting material.
  • Air out a closed attic, garage or crawl space for at least 30 minutes before you go in to work.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Bag the waste, seal the bag, and put it in the trash. Do not handle live or dead rodents with bare hands.
  • Any fever, aches or breathing trouble after handling rodent waste should be checked by a doctor. We do not make health claims about individual exposure.

Sources and standards

Public guidance behind the advice on this page. It describes general practice and rules, not a claim about any one provider.

Common questions

Can you seal an old building without changing how it looks?

Usually yes. We use mesh behind trim and matched patches. We ask before any visible change.

Do neighbors need to cooperate?

It helps a lot. Shared walls and roofs let rodents move between units, so coordinated work lasts longer.

Is foam a good sealant?

Not on its own. Rodents chew it, and it can trap moisture. Metal and masonry materials last longer.
