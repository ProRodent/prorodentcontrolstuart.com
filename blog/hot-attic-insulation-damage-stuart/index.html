Most Stuart homeowners rarely visit their attic. It is hot, dusty and not much fun to explore. That is exactly why problems build there unnoticed. A family of roof rats can live over the bedrooms for months while the household hears an occasional scratch and assumes it is the house settling.

This guide explains what the heat and humidity do to rodent damage, what the insulation, ducts and wiring look like when they have been used as a nest, and why early action matters. It is written for homeowners and avoids drama. The facts are enough.

Why the climate makes a difference.

Attic temperatures in Florida can climb well above the outdoor air in summer, often reaching the range where working up there for long is unsafe. Humidity stays high, even with ventilation. Those two conditions speed up the breakdown of anything organic.

Droppings and urine soaked into insulation decompose faster in heat, releasing a strong ammonia smell. Moisture encourages mold. Damp, soiled insulation compresses and loses its insulating value. In a cooler climate the same mess might stay dry and odorless for years, but here it tends to announce itself.

Heat also affects the animals. Rodents seek shelter, but in the extreme heat they move to cooler parts of the attic, such as near the eaves or around ducts. Cool air leaking from a duct can attract them to a spot that is surprisingly comfortable.

What happens to insulation.

Blown-in insulation is easy for a rodent to dig into. A rat will tunnel through it, compress it into runs and create a nest in the middle. You may see trails, flattened areas and dark patches where urine and droppings have accumulated. Batt insulation gets torn and shredded for nesting.

Insulation that is soiled or compacted does not insulate. In a climate where cooling runs most of the year, that matters for comfort and for the electric bill. A patchy attic may show up as a hot room or a cooling system that cannot keep up.

Light contamination can sometimes be cleaned in place. Heavy contamination, wet areas and anywhere that an animal has died usually calls for removal and replacement. Our attic and crawl space cleanup page explains how that is done safely.

Ducts and the air handler.

In many Florida homes the air handler and duct runs sit in the attic. Flexible ducts are wrapped in insulation and a thin outer jacket. Rodents chew the jacket to reach the insulation, and they sometimes chew through the duct itself. A torn duct leaks conditioned air into the attic, wasting energy and cooling the attic instead of the house.

A damaged duct can also pull attic air into the system, carrying dust and odor into the living space. Rodents also nest near the air handler for warmth and make a mess around the unit. If you notice a musty smell when the system starts, or rooms that do not cool properly, a rodent may be involved.

Wiring and fire risk.

The most serious damage is to wiring. Rodents chew on cable insulation, and exposed conductors can short, arc or overheat. Fires from chewed wiring are a recognized risk. Because attics are hard to inspect, damage can go unnoticed for a long time.

  • Flickering lights or breakers that trip for no clear reason.
  • A burning smell, which should be treated as urgent.
  • Gnaw marks or bare copper visible on cables near the hatch.
  • Outlets or fixtures that stop working in one part of the house.
  • Droppings and shredded material close to junction boxes.

If you see exposed wiring, do not touch it, and do not stay in the attic. Call an electrician, and tell us about it so the plan can include the location. A burning smell calls for turning off the power at the breaker and calling for help right away.

How to look from the hatch.

You can check a lot without going in. Wear a mask and gloves, open the hatch and shine a flashlight around. Look for droppings on the insulation, shredded or flattened areas, nesting material and any daylight that should not be there. Smell the air. A sharp, ammonia-like smell means urine, and a sweet, rotten smell may mean a dead animal.

Take photographs with your phone and do not climb into the attic if it is very hot. A technician has the equipment and the training to do it safely. If you have a roof access issue, such as a tile roof with no easy hatch, mention it so we can plan.

Look outside as well. Check the roofline, the gable vents and the soffits for gaps. The entry point matters more than what is inside, since closing it prevents a return.

When to call about the attic.

  • You hear movement overhead more than once.
  • You see droppings or shredded material from the hatch.
  • A room cools poorly or the air has a musty smell.
  • You notice flickering lights or a tripped breaker.
  • A smell near the ceiling does not fade.

Any one of these is a reason to call. Two or more is a strong reason. The earlier we look, the more of the insulation and wiring can be saved.

The order of work.

The right order is to remove the animals, seal the entry points and then clean. Cleaning first only gives a new group fresh material. We start with rodent trapping and removal, then exclusion and entry point sealing, and then cleanup if it is needed.

Replacement insulation, if needed, goes in after cleanup. It restores comfort and efficiency, and new insulation can be installed with the sealed envelope in mind. We give a price for each step before work begins, so you can choose what to do.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

These steps follow CDC cleanup guidance for rodent droppings and nests. They are general safety practice for any household.

  • Skip the broom and the vacuum on dry droppings. Stirring them up is how dust gets airborne.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Wet droppings with a disinfectant or a bleach solution and let it soak for about five minutes before wiping with paper towels.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Sources and standards

Public guidance behind the advice on this page. It describes general practice and rules, not a claim about any one provider.

Common questions

Can I go up and clean the attic myself?

Only for small areas, with a respirator, gloves and the CDC wet-cleanup method. Heat makes it risky, so large jobs are best left to professionals.

Will new insulation fix the problem?

Not alone. Animals must be removed and entry points sealed first, or new insulation becomes a new nest.

How do I know if wiring is chewed?

You may not. Look near the hatch for gnaw marks, and watch for flickering lights or tripped breakers. An electrician can inspect.
