Rainy Season and Rodents in Stuart: Why Calls Rise After Heavy Rain.

From late spring into fall, afternoon downpours change where rodents live. The shift usually ends at your foundation.

South Florida has two seasons that matter to most residents, and the wet one runs roughly from late May through October. Daily thunderstorms, saturated ground and high humidity define it. For people, it means umbrellas and cancelled plans. For rodents, it means a change of address.

Pest control calls tend to rise during and after the wet months, and the reason is simple. Water forces animals out of the places they normally live. This guide explains the mechanisms, what you may notice around your home and what to do about it before the next storm cell arrives.

How rain moves rodents.

Norway rats dig burrows in soil beside slabs, under sheds and beneath dense groundcover. Those burrows are cool, hidden and safe in dry weather. In a downpour, they flood. An animal caught in rising water will leave, and it heads for the nearest dry, sheltered spot.

The nearest dry spot is often a garage, a shed or the crawl space of a house. Even where a burrow does not flood, saturated ground makes digging harder and drives animals to use existing structures instead. Roof rats are less affected by flooding since they live above ground, but heavy rain soaks palm crowns and nests, and the animals look for a drier roost in an attic.

Mice live inside walls and stored materials, so they are less tied to ground conditions. Even so, outdoor mice nesting in landscaping move indoors when beds are soaked. The same gap that admits a wet mouse also admits a wet rat, so a house with open entries sees more of both.

Signs you may notice.

Look for fresh activity within a few days of a heavy rain event. New droppings along the garage wall, a gnawed bag of pet food, shredded cardboard in a closet or a damp, musty smell near a utility room are common. Scratching in the attic after a storm may mean roof rats sought shelter from a soaked palm.

  • Fresh droppings in a garage, shed or pool equipment area.
  • Muddy tracks or wet paw marks along a slab edge or door threshold.
  • Burrow openings that were not there before, with fresh soil.
  • Gnawed pet food bags, bird seed or stored cardboard.
  • New sounds in walls or ceilings in the days after a storm.
  • Rats seen on fences and wires in daylight, which suggests displacement.

Daytime sightings deserve a note. Rats are mostly nocturnal, so seeing one in the daylight after a downpour often means its nest was flooded or disturbed. It does not necessarily mean the population has grown. Write down what you saw and where, and mention it on the call.

Where the water meets the house.

Drainage affects pest pressure. If water pools along a foundation, it softens soil and encourages burrowing nearby. Downspouts that discharge at the base of the wall, irrigation heads that spray the stucco and low spots in the yard all keep the ground wet. Wet ground along the house is an invitation.

Check that gutters and downspouts carry water away. Extend downspouts several feet from the wall where possible. Regrade low spots if you can. Keep mulch pulled back from the foundation, since thick, damp mulch is a favorite cover for rats and mice.

Humidity inside the house changes too. Damp attics and crawl spaces grow mold and rot wood, and soft, rotted wood is easy to gnaw. A small leak that dampens a soffit can turn a solid board into a place a rat can chew through in an evening. Fix leaks early.

Preparing the house for the wet months.

A pre-season check is straightforward. Walk the roofline and look for tile ends with gaps, loose soffit panels and gable vents without mesh. Walk the base of the walls and look for gaps around pipes, line sets and the dryer vent. Check the garage door seal. Our exclusion and entry point sealing page lists the materials that hold up in humid conditions.

Steel wool and foam fail when wet. Rust takes the first, and rodents chew the second. Stainless mesh, copper mesh, flashing and masonry sealant keep their form. If you use sealant, choose one rated for outdoor use and apply it to a dry surface.

Inside the garage, lift stored boxes off the floor and use plastic bins. A flooded driveway can push water under a garage door, and cardboard turns to mush and a nest in an afternoon. Keep a clear gap along the walls so that you can see droppings and so that traps have room.

After a heavy rain event.

A quick walk-around after a downpour.

  • Check the base of walls and sheds for new burrows or soil mounds.
  • Look at the garage corners and door threshold for droppings or tracks.
  • Listen at dusk for movement in the attic or ceiling.
  • Check that gutters and downspouts are flowing and not pooling by the wall.
  • Pick up soaked fruit, seed or cardboard that is now an easy meal.

If you find signs, act early. A few days after a rain is a good time to call, because the animals have just arrived and have not yet settled in. A short visit then can stop a problem before it spreads.

A simple wet-season routine.

  • Walk the foundation after the first big storm each week.
  • Keep gutters and downspouts clear and pointed away from walls.
  • Pull mulch back from the base of walls.
  • Keep stored items off garage floors.
  • Check the roofline for gaps after wind.

Combine the routine with a regular chore, such as taking out the trash. The habit is more important than the schedule.

What a visit looks like in wet weather.

We inspect the exterior and the interior spaces involved, with a focus on the transition zone where yard meets house. Traps and covered stations go along the routes the animals use, and we shelter them from rain. We check them again after the next storm, since downpours wash away scent and disturb placement.

The plan usually combines rodent trapping and removal with sealing. We do not seal before the animals are out, since closing a gap on an animal that is still inside leads to odor or damage. After trapping, we close the openings and return to confirm that activity has stopped.

What to do, and not do, while you wait

This is general public-health guidance from the CDC, not a description of any one job. It applies whether you call us or anyone else.

  • Do not sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nests. That can put contaminated dust in the air you breathe.
  • Open doors or vents and let an enclosed space air out for 30 minutes or more before cleanup.
  • Gloves on first, handwashing last. Do both every time.
  • Spray the area with disinfectant, wait roughly five minutes, then wipe it up with paper towels instead of dry material.
  • Seal used paper towels and any dead rodent in a bag before disposal. Keep bare skin out of it.
  • Flu-like symptoms or shortness of breath after rodent exposure are a reason to see a doctor and mention the cleanup. Health questions belong with a physician, not a pest company.

Common questions

Why do I see rats during the day after a storm?

A flooded or disturbed nest can push them out. It does not always mean the population has grown, but it is a good time to call.

Does mulch attract rodents?

Thick, damp mulch against the foundation gives them cover. Pull it back a few inches from the wall.

Should I wait until the rainy season ends to call?

No. Activity that starts in the wet season usually grows. Early action is cheaper and faster.
