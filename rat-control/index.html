Two rats, two different jobs.

Two species cause most of the rat calls in Stuart, and treating them the same way wastes time. Roof rats, also called black rats, are slender climbers with long tails. They live up high, in palm crowns, dense vines and attics. Norway rats are heavier and prefer the ground, digging burrows beside slabs, under sheds and along fence bases.

A sound overhead at dusk, with light running along the ceiling, points to roof rats. A burrow opening the size of a golf ball beside a deck or slab, or a worn path along a wall, points to Norway rats. We confirm in person because the traps, the placement and the sealing plan all depend on the answer.

Why Stuart attracts roof rats.

Stuart sits on the St. Lucie River with the Atlantic close by, and the landscaping reflects that. Palms, mature hedges, fruit trees and wooded edges give roof rats food, shelter and an overhead highway to rooftops. A branch touching a tile roof, or a palm frond brushing a wall, is a bridge.

Fruit that falls and is left to rot, such as citrus, mango and avocado, feeds them. So do bird feeders, pet food left outside and unsecured trash. Waterfront and canal-side lots often see more activity because vegetation along the water gives rats corridors that run for blocks.

Where a lot borders water, mangrove or a wooded edge, the question that matters is what connects that cover to your roof. A continuous hedge line, a palm touching an eave or a fence run that reaches the house is how a rat travels from the habitat to the attic, so we map that connection during the inspection and break it first.

What the inspection checks.

  • The roofline, where branches, wires and palm fronds make contact.
  • Tile ends, ridge caps and gable vents where gaps open.
  • Attic insulation for runs, droppings and nesting material.
  • Burrows, rub marks and chewed fruit around the yard.
  • Sheds, pool equipment pads and stacked materials.

Rub marks are greasy dark smears along rafters, pipes and fence tops, left by oily fur on regular routes. Their position tells us where the animals enter and where to concentrate traps.

Trim the bridge, seal the gap, then trap what is left inside.

Trapping and removal.

Roof rats are wary of new objects, so traps often sit unbaited for a day or two so the rats get used to them. Then they are set along the rafters and runs we found. For Norway rats, we place traps against the burrow edge or in tight tunnels along walls. Details are on our rodent trapping and removal page.

Federal rules limit what consumers can buy for rat control, and the strongest baits are for professionals. We use tamper-resistant stations only where they make sense, and never as a substitute for finding the entry point.

Sealing the way back in.

A rat can pass through an opening about the size of a quarter. That includes gaps beside roof tiles, uncapped pipe chases, gable vents without mesh and the soffit corners where the roof meets the wall. We close those with hardware cloth, flashing and sealant rated for outdoor use. Exclusion and entry point sealing is usually the bulk of the lasting fix.

Landscape changes support the work. Cut branches back from the roof by several feet, keep palms trimmed so old fronds do not form a nest, and pick up fallen fruit weekly. These steps reduce the pressure that brings rats back.

What helps while you wait for the visit.

Small steps before the visit make the inspection more productive. Pick up fruit and pet food. Bring bird feeders in at night. Close the garage door after dark. Do not trim palms or cut back hedges in the hour before we arrive, since that can scatter animals into the house.

Write down what you have seen, with times. A rat on a wire at dusk, a sound in the attic at two in the morning and droppings under a tree are three separate clues, and together they usually point to the entry. If you have photographs, keep them handy.

Avoid loose bait. Poisoned rats often retreat into walls and ceilings to die, and the smell can last for weeks in Stuart's heat. A trap placed on a confirmed route is cleaner, faster and easier to track.

How long a rat job usually takes.

A typical roof rat job runs in three stages. The inspection and first trap setting happen on the first visit. Follow-up visits over the next one to two weeks clear and reset traps. Sealing is done once catches stop, so animals are not locked in.

Larger or older properties take longer, especially if the roof is steep or the attic is hard to reach. We give you an honest estimate of visits after the inspection, and we tell you if something changes along the way.

Cleanup and follow-up.

After removal, droppings and soiled insulation in the attic may need attention, which is covered under attic and crawl space cleanup. We return to check the traps and confirm the seals are holding.

Rats gnaw wiring, and that is a fire and outage risk. If you see chewed cable in the attic or garage, tell us on the call and do not touch it. We document damage separately so you can plan any repair.

Before we arrive

  • Keep children and pets away from any area with droppings, a nest, or a suspected entry point.

Questions about rat control

How do I know if I have roof rats?

Overhead noise at dusk or night, droppings in the attic or along rafters, and rub marks high on walls and fence tops all suggest roof rats. Norway rats leave burrows and ground-level signs.

Will trimming my palm trees stop the rats?

It helps by removing nesting sites and bridges, but it does not close the gaps they already use. Trimming works best alongside inspection, trapping and sealing.

Are rats in the attic a health concern?

Droppings and urine can carry bacteria, and rats can damage wiring. Avoid disturbing droppings and call for an inspection.
