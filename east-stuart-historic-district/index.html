A historic neighborhood with deep roots.

East Stuart lies east of the railroad tracks and south of downtown. The Florida Department of State describes the East Stuart Historic District as the core of the historic Black community of East Stuart. Before World War II it was the residential, commercial, religious and educational center for Martin County's Black population, and it was one of Stuart's original five wards when the town incorporated in 1914.

The district was added to the National Register of Historic Places, and the neighborhood has worked for years to preserve its buildings and its memory. We write this page with respect for that work. The practical aim is to help residents keep older wood-frame houses sound, which mostly means crawl space vents, pier gaps and pipe chases, covered below, with repairs that leave original features alone.

What older houses give rodents.

Many homes in the district are wood frame, set on piers or raised foundations, with porches added over time. After decades, the joints where porch meets wall, where siding meets sill and where pipes pass through the floor are rarely tight. Those are the places we check first.

Crawl spaces are the main issue. A raised house has a dark, sheltered void under the floor, usually with a few vents. When vent screens rust or tear, rats and mice walk in. From there they follow pipes and wiring up through gaps to the kitchen, bathroom and attic.

  • Foundation vents with torn, missing or rusted screens.
  • Gaps where porches, steps and additions meet the original house.
  • Openings around plumbing and wiring that pass through the floor.
  • Loose or missing skirting under raised porches.
  • Roof edges and gable vents on older roofs.
Under the house is where the problem starts and the proof is hardest to see.

Yards, fruit trees and the rail line.

Yards in the district tend to be established, with mature trees and sometimes fruit trees that drop food in the warm months. Fallen fruit, pet food and open compost feed rats, and thick hedges give them cover along fence lines. The rail corridor nearby adds a long, quiet strip of weeds and rubble that animals use to travel.

We walk the yard as well as the house. Burrows near sheds and fences, rub marks along fence tops and gnawed fruit show where animals live. The yard side of the visit is explained on our backyard and outdoor rodent control page.

Respecting the house while we fix it.

We do not alter original features. Sealing for a historic house uses metal mesh behind existing trim, cement patches in masonry piers and new vent screens that match the opening. Where a repair would change how the house looks, we ask first and explain the alternative. The materials are listed on our exclusion and entry point sealing page.

Some repairs belong to a carpenter or a restoration specialist, such as rotted sill boards or damaged porch framing. We note what we find, so the right person can handle it.

Renters, owners and landlords.

Many households here rent, and many properties have an owner who lives elsewhere. If you rent, tell us, and we explain what you can do from inside and what the owner needs to approve. If you own several houses, we can describe a plan that covers them in turn and record what was done at each address.

Clean-up after heavy activity may include insulation and crawl space work. See attic and crawl space cleanup for how that is done safely.

A simple checklist for older houses.

  • Look at each vent screen under the house and note any tears.
  • Check where porch steps meet the wall for gaps.
  • Walk the fence line and look for burrows or rub marks.
  • Pick up fallen fruit and bring pet food in at night.
  • Listen at dusk for movement in the attic or ceiling.

A few minutes a month is enough to spot early signs. The sooner a gap is found, the smaller and cheaper the repair.

Before we arrive

  • Close off the room with the most activity if you can, and keep doors shut behind you.
  • Avoid stirring up dust in attics or closets before we arrive.
  • Do not poke at nests or burrows, which can scatter the animals into new areas.
  • Photograph droppings, gnawing or damage with your phone so we can see what you saw.
